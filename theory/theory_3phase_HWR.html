Three Phase Half-Wave Controlled Rectifier — R Load & R-L Load
SCR-based controlled rectification · Three-phase supply · Firing angle α · Thyristors T1, T2, T3 · 50 Hz system
i Introduction

A three-phase half-wave controlled rectifier is an electrical circuit used for converting alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC) by controlling the flow of current through a load. It is called a half-wave rectifier because it rectifies only one half of each input waveform per phase.

The working principle can be summarised across the following key stages:

1. Input: The rectifier is connected to a three-phase AC power source. The AC voltage waveform consists of three phases: phase A, phase B, and phase C.

2. Rectification: Each phase of the AC source is connected to a thyristor, forming a thyristor bridge. When the input voltage is positive in one phase, the corresponding thyristor conducts provided that a gate trigger is given, allowing current to flow through the load.

3. Control: In a controlled rectifier, the current flow through the thyristor (also known as a Silicon Controlled Rectifier or SCR) is governed by the gate signal. The thyristor acts as a switch that can be turned on or off depending on the control signal applied to its gate.

4. Triggering: Thyristors are triggered at specific points in the input waveform by applying a pulse signal to the gate at the desired firing angle α. The firing angle determines the portion of the input waveform during which the thyristor conducts current.

5. Conduction: Once triggered, the thyristor turns on and allows current to flow through the load until the end of the half-cycle or until the thyristor is turned off.

6. Commutation: When the input waveform crosses zero or becomes negative, natural commutation occurs, turning off the conducting thyristor. This happens because the forward voltage across the thyristor drops below its holding voltage.

7. Output: The load receives a pulsating DC voltage consisting of positive half-cycles. By controlling the firing angle α of the thyristors, the average output voltage can be varied, enabling regulation of the power delivered to the load.

Important Note: The three-phase half-wave controlled rectifier is a simplified configuration and may require additional components — such as filters and smoothing capacitors — to reduce ripple and provide a smoother DC output. Overall, it provides a means to convert three-phase AC power to pulsating DC power by controlling the firing of thyristors in each phase.
1 Three Phase Half Wave Controlled Rectifier with Resistive (R) Load
Fig. 1 — R Load Circuit Diagram
Fig. 1: Three Phase Half Wave Controlled Rectifier with R Load

The circuit consists of a delta–star transformer and three thyristors T1, T2, T3 connected on the secondary star-connected winding and a resistive load. When Va is positive, T1 becomes forward biased and conducts. During the negative cycle of Va, T1 turns off. Similarly, T2 and T3 conduct only during the positive cycles of Vb and Vc respectively.

The average output voltage can be varied by varying the firing angles of the thyristors. In the waveforms below, Va is denoted as Van, Vb as Vbn and Vc as Vcn.

Case 1: α < 30°

For firing angles less than 30°, each thyristor conducts for a full 120° interval and the output voltage waveform has no zero-voltage gaps. The expressions for average and RMS output voltage and current are:

Eq. 1 — Average Output Voltage (α < 30°)
\[ V_o = \frac{1}{\dfrac{2\pi}{3}} \int_{\alpha+\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\alpha+\frac{5\pi}{6}} V_{mp}\sin\omega t\; d(\omega t) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\,V_{mp}\cos\alpha = \frac{3}{2\pi}\,V_{ml}\cos\alpha \]
Eq. 2 — Average Output Current (α < 30°)
\[ I_o = \frac{V_o}{R} = \frac{3}{2\pi R}\,V_{ml}\cos\alpha \]
Eq. 3 — RMS Output Voltage (α < 30°)
\[ V_{o\,(rms)} = \sqrt{3}\,V_{mp} \left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} = V_{ml}\left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} \]
Eq. 4 — RMS Output Current (α < 30°)
\[ I_{o\,rms} = \frac{V_{o\,rms}}{R} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,V_{mp}}{R} \left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} \]
Case 2: α > 30°

For firing angles greater than 30°, the output voltage waveform contains zero-voltage gaps during which all thyristors are off. The upper limit of integration reduces to π (end of positive half-cycle):

Eq. 5 — Average Output Voltage (α > 30°)
\[ V_o = \frac{1}{\dfrac{2\pi}{3}} \int_{\alpha+\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\pi} V_{mp}\sin\omega t\; d(\omega t) = \frac{3}{2\pi}\,V_{mp}\!\left[1 + \cos(\alpha + 30^\circ)\right] \]
Eq. 6 — Average Output Current (α > 30°)
\[ I_o = \frac{V_o}{R} = \frac{3}{2\pi R}\,V_{mp}\!\left[1 + \cos(\alpha + 30^\circ)\right] \]
Eq. 7 — RMS Output Voltage (α > 30°)
\[ V_{o\,(rms)} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,V_{mp}}{2\sqrt{\pi}} \left[\left(\frac{5\pi}{6} - \alpha\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sin\!\left(2\alpha + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right]^{1/2} \]
Eq. 8 — RMS Output Current (α > 30°)
\[ I_{o\,(rms)} = \frac{V_{o\,(rms)}}{R} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,V_{mp}}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,R} \left[\left(\frac{5\pi}{6} - \alpha\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sin\!\left(2\alpha + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right]^{1/2} \]
2 Three Phase Half Wave Controlled Rectifier with R-L Load
Fig. 1 — R-L Load Circuit Diagram
Fig. 1: Three Phase Half Wave Controlled Rectifier with R-L Load

The circuit consists of a delta–star transformer and three thyristors T1, T2, T3 connected on the secondary star-connected winding and an R-L load.

When Va is positive, T1 becomes forward biased and conducts. During the negative cycle of Va, the current through T1 is not zero due to the inductor in the load — so T1 remains ON during the negative cycle of Va. When Vb is positive, T2 is triggered and the load current is transferred from T1 to T2; at this instant T1 turns OFF.

Similarly, during the negative cycle of Vb, T2 remains ON due to the inductor. When T3 is triggered during the positive cycle of Vc, the load current is transferred from T2 to T3, and T2 turns OFF. T3 conducts during the negative cycle of Vc and turns OFF when T1 is triggered again. The average output voltage can be varied by varying the firing angles of the thyristors.

Fig. 2 — R-L Load Waveforms
Fig. 2: input voltage, output voltage, load current, load voltage, voltage across thyristors T1, T2 and T3 (VT1, VT2 and VT3), gate pulse (VGP1, VGP2, VGP3)

For the R-L load, due to the continuous current conduction maintained by the inductance, the integration limits span the full 120° interval for all values of α. The average and RMS output voltage and current are:

Eq. 9 — Average Output Voltage (R-L Load)
\[ V_o = \frac{1}{\dfrac{2\pi}{3}} \int_{\alpha+\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\alpha+\frac{5\pi}{6}} V_{mp}\sin\omega t\; d(\omega t) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\,V_{mp}\cos\alpha = \frac{3}{2\pi}\,V_{ml}\cos\alpha \]
Eq. 10 — Average Output Current (R-L Load)
\[ I_o = \frac{V_o}{R} = \frac{3}{2\pi R}\,V_{ml}\cos\alpha \]
Eq. 11 — RMS Output Voltage (R-L Load)
\[ V_{o\,(rms)} = \sqrt{3}\,V_{mp} \left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} = V_{ml}\left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} \]
Eq. 12 — RMS Output Current (R-L Load)
\[ I_{o\,rms} = \frac{V_{o\,rms}}{R} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,V_{mp}}{R} \left[\frac{1}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\cos 2\alpha\right]^{1/2} \]
3 Advantages, Disadvantages & Applications
Advantages
  1. High efficiency in AC-to-DC power conversion — reduced power loss by utilizing only one half-cycle per phase.
  2. Reduced ripple content due to the balanced three-phase waveform, improving output voltage stability.
  3. Lower harmonic distortion compared to single-phase rectifiers, contributing to improved power quality.
  4. Capable of handling higher power loads — suitable for industrial motor drives, EV power supplies, and large-scale battery charging.
  5. Improved reliability through three-phase redundancy — if one phase fails, the other two continue to deliver power.
  6. Better power factor correction capabilities compared to single-phase rectifiers, reducing reactive power and improving energy efficiency.
Disadvantages
  1. Only half of each input waveform is utilised, resulting in a lower average output voltage compared to full-wave rectifiers.
  2. Significant harmonic distortion in the rectified output, increasing EMI and reducing power quality.
  3. Discontinuous current drawn from the AC supply causes poor power factor and increased line losses.
  4. Not suitable for driving inductive loads without freewheeling diodes — the intermittent output restricts applicability.
  5. Precise voltage regulation is challenging, especially under varying load conditions.
  6. High-voltage transients during thyristor switching increase stress on components, reducing reliability.
  7. Additional control circuitry for firing-angle management adds complexity and cost.
  8. Reduced overall efficiency compared to full-wave topologies, leading to increased power losses.
Applications
  1. Motor Drives — speed control of DC motors in industrial applications.
  2. Welding Equipment — providing controlled DC power for arc welding machines.
  3. Power Quality Control — harmonic mitigation and reactive power compensation systems.
  4. Battery Charging Systems — high-power charging for industrial and EV battery banks.
  5. Power Supplies — DC power supplies for electrochemical processes, electrolysis, and plating.
